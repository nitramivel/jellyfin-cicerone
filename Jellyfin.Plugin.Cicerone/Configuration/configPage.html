Cicerone

Checks that a subtitle track is the language it claims to be and is timed to the dialogue it sits over — by comparing when the subtitles say somebody is speaking against when the audio says so. That costs nothing and runs locally. A transcriber is needed only to write a track for something that has none.
Cicerone needs a timed transcript, not a good one — the whole output is a comparison between when a word was said and when the subtitle claims it was. That rules out more models than it sounds like it should: gpt-4o-transcribe and its mini return an excellent transcript with no timings in it and are useless here. whisper-1 is the right choice, and happens to be the cheap one.

Anything speaking OpenAI's /audio/transcriptions endpoint works — Speaches, faster-whisper-server, whisper.cpp's server, LM Studio, Groq. Pointed at a local one, no audio leaves your network and the whole thing is free.

Free, and it runs first. A track tagged English and written in Spanish is a common and completely invisible fault — the player shows the language you picked, the words are wrong, and no amount of sync checking explains it. Asking a model would cost a call per track to answer the one question here that a few hundred bytes of table answers exactly, so it is answered locally, for nothing, before any audio is touched.

It is deliberately reluctant: the tag has to be beaten by a clear margin, not merely beaten, and an untagged track is never contradicted at all because it never made a claim.
One measurement is worse than none. A single-point check reports a frame-rate-mismatched file as perfect, because it is perfect wherever you happened to look — and now you believe it. Two measurements is the minimum that can see a drift at all; a dozen is what makes the answer survive several of them landing in a silence.
Sync is a question about when somebody spoke, not about what they said. Speech activity answers it by finding every stretch of the film that is not silence and sliding the subtitle track over it until the two agree — no model, no network, no bill, and it reads the entire file rather than the few windows a per-item charge would stretch to. It is both the cheaper option and the better evidence.

The transcript method is kept for the one thing it can still do better: telling two films apart by their words rather than by the rhythm of their dialogue. It costs a few minutes of audio per item, every item, forever.

The most common way a subtitle file is wrong, and the hardest to see. A file timed against a 25 fps PAL transfer, played over a 23.976 fps source, is perfect on the first line and four minutes adrift by the end. Anyone who has nudged an offset until the opening scene matched and found it broken again an hour later has met this.

A measured drift of 1.0427 is not a number worth keeping when what it means is 25/23.976. Snapping to the exact ratio makes a repair correct for the whole runtime instead of accumulating the fit's own error across three hours, and it lets the report name the fault rather than print a slope.
All three are resampled to 16 kHz mono first, which is what speech models are trained on and what they would resample to anyway. Opus at 16 kbit/s is about 60 KB for a thirty-second window and transparent for speech. Choose WAV for a local server that would rather not decode.
Everything one item has by way of subtitles, in one place: the tracks inside the file, the sidecars beside it, and anything Cicerone has written itself. Save a copy of any of them, nudge the timing, edit the text, or delete the ones that have accumulated.

Cicerone never overwrites a file it did not write. Editing somebody else's track saves a copy and leaves the original alone; editing one of Cicerone's own saves over it, because putting that back costs a re-run. Deleting is the exception, and deliberately so — a manager exists so you can remove the four subtitle files that have piled up beside a film, and it asks first.

Cicerone never edits a subtitle file. A correction is a measurement, measurements have error, and the failure mode of editing in place is destroying a file somebody spent an evening timing by hand. A repair is always a new file beside the original, named so that Jellyfin reads the language back out of it and so that nothing here will ever overwrite a file it did not write.
Reporting only is the default. Look at what a first run finds before letting it write anything — a library where every item reads as mismatched usually means a misconfigured transcriber, not two hundred broken subtitle files.
